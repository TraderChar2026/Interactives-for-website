Feed the Microbiome

A probiotic capsule delivers live bacteria — usually Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species. A prebiotic delivers the fermentable fiber those bacteria feed on: inulin, resistant starch, beta-glucan, galacto-oligosaccharides. "Feeding" here means exactly that — fiber that survives digestion in the small intestine, reaches the colon intact, and gets fermented by bacteria there, producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs): acetate, propionate and butyrate. Colonocytes — the cells lining the colon — run on butyrate. It supplies roughly 70% of their energy. Starve them of butyrate and the gut barrier weakens.

Give the probiotic without the fiber and the bacteria arrive with no fiber to consume. They pass through instead of establishing, and SCFA production never rises. Colonocytes lack butyrate, the gut barrier keeps weakening, and the sugar-fed bacteria you were trying to displace continue to multiply.

The clinical point

Probiotics are among the most commonly recommended supplements in practice, and they are frequently recommended alone. With no fiber to consume, the probiotic bacteria have no advantage over the established microflora. Colonization is transient, and the SCFA signaling that makes the microbiome clinically relevant never begins.

That signaling is what matters at the cellular level. Fermentable fibers produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) — acetate, propionate and butyrate. These activate two cell-surface receptors, GPR41 and GPR43 (G-protein-coupled receptors 41 and 43), found on intestinal cells, fat cells and immune cells. That receptor activation drives secretion of PYY (peptide YY, a satiety hormone) and GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1, the same incretin hormone targeted by GLP-1 agonist medications). Butyrate is also the primary fuel for colonocytes — the cells lining the colon — and helps maintain the tight junctions that keep the gut barrier intact.

SCFAs also feed into the antioxidant pathways covered elsewhere on this site. Butyrate and propionate can activate Keap1-Nrf2 signaling (Nrf2 is the transcription factor that switches on the cell's own antioxidant enzymes; Keap1 is the protein that holds it inactive until stress releases it). Separately, bacteria convert dietary tryptophan into indole compounds that activate AhR (the aryl hydrocarbon receptor), which provides a second route to Nrf2 activation in the liver.

What "opportunistic" means here. These are ordinary members of the established microflora in a healthy gut, present at low abundance: Enterobacteriaceae such as Escherichia coli and Klebsiella, along with Enterococcus species and Clostridioides difficile. They need no complex fiber. They grow fast on simple sugars, tolerate oxygen, and multiply whenever conditions favor them. Their outer membrane carries lipopolysaccharide (LPS), an endotoxin that triggers inflammation once it crosses a weakened barrier.

Refined carbohydrate starves the fiber-fermenting bacteria. The small intestine absorbs sugar and white flour before they reach the colon, so little fermentable fiber arrives. Butyrate production falls, and a self-reinforcing loop begins: colonocytes normally burn butyrate through beta-oxidation, and that oxygen consumption keeps the colon anaerobic. With less butyrate, luminal oxygen rises — which suits oxygen-tolerant Proteobacteria like E. coli and further suppresses butyrate producers, which are oxygen-sensitive. Meanwhile, bacteria starved of dietary fiber consume the mucus layer instead, thinning the barrier separating them from the epithelium.

Fiber first, then the probiotic — or better, both together. Fiber does two things a probiotic cannot: it feeds the bacteria you want and starves the ones you don't. Give a patient a probiotic without changing what they eat and you have dropped probiotic bacteria into a gut with no fiber to feed them — they pass through instead of colonizing — while the sugar-adapted population you were trying to displace keeps growing.

Fecal transplant is the ceiling case, and it makes the same point. FMT transfers an entire donor community rather than a handful of strains, and for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection it works — roughly 90% success in observational series, and it is now an approved therapy. But engraftment varies, and it fades. In one study of encapsulated FMT, 22% of patients showed declining donor engraftment within about a month, while others retained donor strains at one year and, in long-term follow-up, several years. Recipient diet is one reason: a randomized controlled trial in ulcerative colitis compared FMT with and without psyllium fiber supplementation and found that fiber promoted donor strain engraftment. A patient reporting that a transplant "only lasted a couple of months" is describing something the literature already documents.

Consider what that means. Even a complete community transfer — the largest possible dose of bacteria — engrafts better when fiber is there to sustain it. FMT is a regulated procedure with a narrow approved indication and its own risks, and nothing here suggests any supplement is comparable to it. The bottom line: bacteria persist when fed.

Probiotics have real, strain-specific indications, and this is not an argument against them. It is an argument about pairing them. Send the probiotic in with fiber to consume and its colonies establish and grow; send it in alone and the colonies die off. Worth distinguishing here: fermented foods are probiotic sources, not automatically prebiotic ones. Fermented vegetables like sauerkraut and kimchi carry both, since the bacteria arrive in the plant fiber they fermented. Kefir and yogurt deliver probiotic bacteria with almost no fiber, and still need a prebiotic.

Check that the cultures are alive. Pasteurization after fermentation kills them. Shelf-stable canned or jarred sauerkraut has been heat-treated, so it delivers fiber but no live bacteria — a prebiotic without the probiotic. Many yogurts are heat-treated after culturing for shelf life; the label has to say live and active cultures. Vinegar-brined pickles were never fermented at all. The practical rule for vegetables: if it sits on a shelf at room temperature, the bacteria are dead. Buy it refrigerated.
