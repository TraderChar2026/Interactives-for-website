Oxidative Load Profiler

No single exposure creates oxidative stress. Load accumulates. A patient metabolizing normally, sleeping five hours, eating processed food, and living beside a freeway carries a different burden than any one of those explains on its own. What to do with this

Underlying oxidative stress levels. Mitochondria generate reactive oxygen species during normal respiration. Every patient carries this baseline, it never reaches zero, and it rises with age as mitochondrial efficiency declines. No intervention removes it.

Most of what sits on top of that baseline is modifiable. Smoking, alcohol, ultra-processed diet, short sleep, and untreated metabolic dysfunction usually dominate a profile, and each one responds to intervention. That is the useful finding when you build a profile with a patient: the largest bars are almost always the ones they can change.

Exercise behaves differently from everything else here. A bout of intense exercise generates reactive oxygen species acutely. Regular moderate training prompts the cell to manufacture more superoxide dismutase, catalase and glutathione-related enzymes, and that standing enzyme supply outweighs the acute cost of any single session. This is hormesis: a stressor that provokes the cell into building the defenses it needs. The profiler credits regular training for that reason, not activity in general.

Intensity and training status decide which way it goes. In aged mice, a single bout of high-intensity endurance stress left the animals more susceptible to oxidative damage, while six weeks of moderate training produced the opposite result: higher antioxidant enzyme levels and better control of oxidative damage. A patient who never exercises and then spends a weekend shoveling snow or moving furniture takes the oxidative hit without increasing antioxidant enzymes. Each session of regular moderate exercise activates Nrf2, and the cell responds by manufacturing more superoxide dismutase and catalase. That standing enzyme level climbs over weeks of repeated sessions. One hard weekend does not raise it.

Age blunts the exercise response, and this matters for older patients. When researchers compared 30 minutes of cycling in men aged 23 and men aged 63, nuclear Nrf2 rose significantly in the young group but not the older one, and downstream antioxidant gene expression (HMOX1, NQO1) increased in the young while being repressed in the older adults. The phenomenon has been described as exercise desensitization. It is partly reversible: a randomized trial in adults 60 and over found eight weeks of aerobic training (3 days a week, 45 minutes) partially restored the Nrf2 response. So the prescription for an older patient is sustained regular training rather than occasional exertion — and the expected return per session is smaller than it would be for a 30-year-old.

This is also where activation and exercise may be complementary rather than competing. A 2025 study in adults averaging 67 combined acute exercise with sulforaphane and found greater NRF2 signaling than either produced alone. The population in which exercise-induced Nrf2 activation works least well is the population in which an exogenous activator appears to contribute most — which lines up with the age-dependent signal seen in the supplement trials. This is an emerging line of work, not settled practice.

Ranking a patient's exposures gives you something concrete to discuss and a defensible place to start. It does not diagnose, quantify, or predict.
