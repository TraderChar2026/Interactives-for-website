Vitamin C vs. Superoxide Dismutase

Both handle superoxide. They do it by completely different mechanisms. Vitamin C works stoichiometrically — one molecule neutralizes roughly one radical and is oxidized in the process. Superoxide dismutase works catalytically — one enzyme dismutates radical after radical, near the diffusion limit, without being consumed. Run both against the same superoxide load.

Why this matters clinically

The gap isn't a matter of dose. It's a difference in kind. Vitamin C can only ever neutralize what it stoichiometrically matches, and then it is oxidized and needs regenerating. Superoxide dismutase is among the fastest enzymes ever characterized, operating close to the rate at which molecules can physically diffuse into contact with it — and it comes out of every cycle intact, ready for the next.

~100 : 1
Inside a cell, ascorbate molecules (~109) outnumber SOD molecules (~107) by roughly a hundred to one.
~106/sec
Turnover of a single SOD molecule. Ascorbate turns over once, then is oxidized.
~100 µs
Time for the cell's SOD to clear as many radicals as its entire ascorbate pool could handle in total.

This is the mechanistic case for interest in inducing the body's own antioxidant enzymes rather than supplying antioxidants directly, and it helps explain why large trials of direct antioxidant supplementation have so often returned null results. It is a statement about mechanism only. Whether a given intervention meaningfully induces these enzymes in humans, and whether that produces measurable clinical benefit, is a separate question — and one where the human trial evidence is mixed, including two randomized placebo-controlled trials that found no significant effect. The evidence pages address that directly.
